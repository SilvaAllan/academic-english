Ideas Night · "A Wrong Answer Is Information"

LH
Dr Leila Haddad
Educational psychologist · Ideas Night talk · 5 min

Ideas Night is an evening of short talks at a university. Leila tells the story of the moment that shaped her career, and the research that explains it.

First listening · the big idea

What is Leila's main message?

1When people ask me what made me who I am, they expect a story about talent. They want to hear that I was a brilliant child who loved books. But that's not true. What really shaped me wasn't talent. It was a red pen.

2I grew up in Marseille, in a small flat above my parents' bakery. My upbringing was simple: we worked, we ate together, and we didn't talk much about school. Nobody in my family had been to university. So when I failed a maths exam at fourteen, nobody was surprised. I wasn't either. I had decided I just wasn't "a maths person".

3Then I met Madame Roux, my new maths teacher. She gave me back my exam covered in red ink. But it wasn't my mistakes that she had circled. It was the questions I had left blank. Next to each one, she wrote the same thing: "Try. A wrong answer is information."

4In hindsight, that was the most pivotal moment of my education. Madame Roux never praised me for being clever. She praised me for trying, for asking questions, for coming back after a bad result. What she was doing, I realised much later, was teaching me resilience. She became my first real role model.

5Years later, as a psychology student, I discovered that there was research behind her red pen. In 1998, Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck ran a series of studies with ten- and eleven-year-old children in the United States. After a first set of problems, some children were praised for their intelligence: "You must be smart at this." Others were praised for their effort: "You must have worked hard." Then all the children faced much harder problems, and they failed. The results were striking. It was the children praised for effort who kept trying and still enjoyed the problems. The children praised for intelligence enjoyed them less, gave up sooner and did worse on the next test.

6That's why I became a researcher. The reason I study motivation today is that I know what one comment can do. So think about the beliefs you have about what you can and cannot do. Who instilled them in you? For most of us, it's the people around us in our formative years who have the most profound impact on us: a parent, a coach, a teacher with a red pen.

7I don't attribute my career to talent. I attribute it to one teacher who fostered curiosity instead of fear. The thing that changed my life wasn't a lesson about numbers. It was a lesson about mistakes. And all I needed was someone to tell me that a wrong answer is information. Thank you.

Compare the plain sentence with Leila's. What changes?

  1. Do the two versions give different information, or the same information with a different focus?
  2. Where is the important new idea in Leila's versions: at the start or at the end?

It-clefts
startIt + befocusthe key idealinkwho / thatrestthe rest

It was my maths teacher who changed my mind.
It was in Marseille that I grew up.
It wasn't talent that made the difference. It was practice.

Use who or that for people, that for everything else, including times and places. Never drop It.

What-clefts
startWhat + clauseverbis / wasfocusthe key idea

What really shaped me was a red pen.
What I need is more time.
What she did was (to) praise my effort.

The key idea comes last, where listeners expect new information. Don't add it: What I need it is…

Other frames

The thing that… was… · The person who… was… · The reason (why)… is that… · All I wanted was… · It was not until… that…

We use all these frames to emphasise, to contrast (not X, but Y) and to correct someone.

Think like an academic · Emphasis in research writing

Leila's article starts like this. Why does she use these frames?

Introduction · paragraph 1

Praise is often seen as a simple way to motivate students. However, it is not praise itself that matters, but the type of praise students receive. In a series of six studies, Mueller and Dweck (1998) found that children praised for intelligence showed less persistence after failure than children praised for effort. What these findings suggest is that intelligence praise can lead children to see ability as fixed. It is this belief, rather than ability itself, that may explain the drop in performance. More recent research, however, has found much smaller effects of mindset programmes in real classrooms (Sisk et al., 2018). What remains unclear is which students benefit most, and under what conditions.

Illustrative paragraph. Mueller & Dweck (1998) and Sisk et al. (2018) are real studies.

What does each highlighted frame do?

Frames for academic writing

Correct or contrast: It is not X but Y that… · It is X, rather than Y, that…

Interpret: What these findings suggest / show is that…

Point to a gap: What remains unclear is… · What has not yet been studied is…

History of an idea: It was not until the 1990s that researchers began to…

## 0. The lesson talk: Ideas Night, "A Wrong Answer Is Information"

Context: Ideas Night is an evening of short talks at a university. Dr Leila Haddad, an educational psychologist, tells the story of the moment that shaped her career. (Leila and Ideas Night are fictional. The study she describes is real: Mueller & Dweck, 1998.)

1. When people ask me what made me who I am, they expect a story about talent. They want to hear that I was a brilliant child who loved books. But that's not true. What really shaped me wasn't talent. It was a red pen.

2. I grew up in Marseille, in a small flat above my parents' bakery. My upbringing was simple: we worked, we ate together, and we didn't talk much about school. Nobody in my family had been to university. So when I failed a maths exam at fourteen, nobody was surprised. I wasn't either. I had decided I just wasn't "a maths person".

3. Then I met Madame Roux, my new maths teacher. She gave me back my exam covered in red ink. But it wasn't my mistakes that she had circled. It was the questions I had left blank. Next to each one, she wrote the same thing: "Try. A wrong answer is information."

4. In hindsight, that was the most pivotal moment of my education. Madame Roux never praised me for being clever. She praised me for trying, for asking questions, for coming back after a bad result. What she was doing, I realised much later, was teaching me resilience. She became my first real role model.

5. Years later, as a psychology student, I discovered that there was research behind her red pen. In 1998, Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck ran a series of studies with ten- and eleven-year-old children in the United States. After a first set of problems, some children were praised for their intelligence: "You must be smart at this." Others were praised for their effort: "You must have worked hard." Then all the children faced much harder problems, and they failed. The results were striking. It was the children praised for effort who kept trying and still enjoyed the problems. The children praised for intelligence enjoyed them less, gave up sooner and did worse on the next test.

6. That's why I became a researcher. The reason I study motivation today is that I know what one comment can do. So think about the beliefs you have about what you can and cannot do. Who instilled them in you? For most of us, it's the people around us in our formative years who have the most profound impact on us: a parent, a coach, a teacher with a red pen.

7. I don't attribute my career to talent. I attribute it to one teacher who fostered curiosity instead of fear. The thing that changed my life wasn't a lesson about numbers. It was a lesson about mistakes. And all I needed was someone to tell me that a wrong answer is information. Thank you.

Main message: how important people react to our efforts and mistakes can shape who we become.

### A note on the research

Mueller and Dweck (1998) is a famous and real set of six studies. Later research on "growth mindset" programmes in schools is more mixed: a 2018 review of many studies (Sisk et al., *Psychological Science*) found that the effects in real classrooms were much smaller than many people expected, with some benefits for students at academic risk. Good researchers look at a body of evidence, not just one study.

## 1. Grammar guide: cleft sentences

A cleft sentence takes one sentence and splits it into two parts, to put the **focus** on one idea. The information is the same; the emphasis changes.

- Plain: *A red pen shaped me.*
- Cleft: *What really shaped me was **a red pen**.*

### 1.1 It-clefts

Form: **It + be + FOCUS + who / that + the rest**

- *It was **my maths teacher** who changed my mind.* (a person: who or that)
- *It was **the blank questions** that she circled.* (a thing: that)
- *It was **in Marseille** that I grew up.* (a place: that)
- *It was **in 2015** that I started.* (a time: that)

Use It-clefts to **correct** or **contrast**:

- *It wasn't **talent** that made the difference. It was **practice**.*
- *Actually, it was **my grandmother** who taught me to read, not my mother.*

Special frame: **It was not until + time + that…** (= only then, not before)

- *It was not until 2015 that I started observing real stars.*

### 1.2 What-clefts

Form: **What + subject + verb + is / was + FOCUS**

- *What really shaped me **was** a red pen.*
- *What I need **is** more time.*
- *What she did **was** (to) praise my effort.* (What + do: the focus is an action)

The key idea comes **last**, where listeners expect new information.

### 1.3 Other frames

- *The thing that changed my life was…*
- *The person who taught me most was…*
- *The reason (why) I study motivation is that…*
- *All I wanted was to pass the exam.* (= the only thing)
- *The question I want to ask is this: …*

### 1.4 Clefts in academic writing

Researchers use clefts to guide the reader to the most important idea.

| Function | Frame | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Correct a common belief | It is not X but Y that… | *It is not praise itself that matters, but the type of praise students receive.* |
| Identify a cause | It is X, rather than Y, that… | *It is this belief, rather than ability itself, that may explain the drop in performance.* |
| Interpret findings | What these findings suggest / show is that… | *What these findings suggest is that intelligence praise can lead children to see ability as fixed.* |
| Point to a research gap | What remains unclear is… | *What remains unclear is which students benefit most.* |
| History of an idea | It was not until… that… | *It was not until the 1990s that researchers began to…* |

Model paragraph (illustrative; both studies are real):

> Praise is often seen as a simple way to motivate students. However, it is not praise itself that matters, but the type of praise students receive. In a series of six studies, Mueller and Dweck (1998) found that children praised for intelligence showed less persistence after failure than children praised for effort. What these findings suggest is that intelligence praise can lead children to see ability as fixed. It is this belief, rather than ability itself, that may explain the drop in performance. More recent research, however, has found much smaller effects of mindset programmes in real classrooms (Sisk et al., 2018). What remains unclear is which students benefit most, and under what conditions.

### 1.5 Common mistakes (especially for Portuguese speakers)

Portuguese uses the same idea as the It-cleft all the time, so this structure feels natural. The problem is the form.

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Was my grandfather who taught me. | **It** was my grandfather who taught me. | English sentences need a subject: never drop *It*. |
| What I need it is more time. | What I need **is** more time. | The What-clause is the subject. No extra *it*. |
| It was in 2015 when I started. | It was in 2015 **that** I started. | Formal English prefers *that* for times and places. |
| It was my teacher which helped me. | It was my teacher **who / that** helped me. | *which* is not used for people. |
| The reason is because… | The reason is **that**… | *The reason… is that* is the safest form in academic writing. |

### 1.6 Pronunciation

- Make a small pause after the What-clause: *What really shaped me | was a red PEN.*
- Stress the focus: *It wasn't TALent. It was PRACtice.*
- Moving the stress changes the meaning: *It was my SISTER who taught me English* (not my brother) vs *It was in LONDON that I learned English* (not in Dublin).

## 2. Vocabulary

### Lesson vocabulary: talking about what shaped you

| Expression | Meaning | Pattern / example |
|---|---|---|
| upbringing | the way your parents raised you, and the values you learned as a child | *My upbringing was simple.* a strict / happy / traditional upbringing |
| in hindsight | looking back now, with what you know today | *In hindsight, that was the most pivotal moment.* |
| pivotal | extremely important, because other things depend on it | *a pivotal moment / role / decision* |
| resilience | the ability to recover quickly after problems | *She was teaching me resilience.* build / show resilience; resilient (adj.) |
| a role model | a person you admire and want to be like | *She became my first real role model.* be a role model for someone |
| instil | put an idea, value or feeling into someone's mind, little by little | *Who instilled these beliefs in you?* instil something in someone (US: instill) |
| formative | having a strong influence on how a person develops | *our formative years / a formative experience* |
| have a profound impact on | have a very deep and strong effect on | *Teachers can have a profound impact on children.* |
| attribute X to Y | say that X is the result of Y (formal) | *I attribute my career to one teacher.* |
| foster | help something to develop | *foster curiosity / creativity / a sense of belonging* |

Need a preposition: instil (in), attribute (to), have a profound impact (on). Academic: attribute to, foster, have a profound impact on. Close to *shape* (Lesson 1): instil, foster, have a profound impact on.

### Phrases for a talk

- Opening: *What really shaped me was… · The question I want to ask is… · The thing nobody tells you is…*
- Correcting and contrasting: *It wasn't X. It was Y. · It's not X that matters, it's Y. · Actually, it was… who… · All I wanted was…*
- Ending: *In hindsight, … · I attribute… to… · The reason I… today is that…*

### Phrases for academic emphasis

*What these findings suggest is that… · It is X, rather than Y, that… · What remains unclear is… · It was not until… that…*

## 3. More model texts

### A "Formative Moments" magazine column (model, 140 words)

> What really shaped me wasn't a school or a book. It was a kitchen. I grew up in Belo Horizonte, and my upbringing was loud, busy and full of food. It was my aunt Teresa who instilled a love of science in me, although she never studied it: she was a baker, and every recipe was an experiment. "If it fails, change one thing," she used to say. In hindsight, that was my first lesson in the scientific method. The most pivotal moment came at seventeen, when I failed my first chemistry exam. All I wanted was to quit, but Teresa made me bake the same cake five times until it worked. That taught me resilience. Today I'm a food scientist, and I attribute my career to her. It was in her kitchen that I learned that a failed experiment is just information.

### A short conversation after a talk

**A:** Great talk! So was it your parents who got you interested in psychology?
**B:** No, actually. It was a teacher. My parents were always working.
**A:** And when did you decide to study it?
**B:** It wasn't until my second year at university that I really decided. What I loved was the research methods course.
**A:** Interesting. What do you think teachers should do differently?
**B:** The thing that matters most is how they react to mistakes. That's what children remember.

## 4. Self-check questions (with answers)

1. Correct the sentence: *Was my teacher who helped me.* → **It** was my teacher who helped me.
2. Correct the sentence: *What I need it is practice.* → What I need **is** practice.
3. Rewrite with emphasis on the place: *I grew up in Recife.* → It was in Recife that I grew up.
4. Rewrite with a What-cleft: *I wanted a quiet place to study.* → What I wanted was a quiet place to study.
5. Rewrite with All: *I only needed some time.* → All I needed was some time.
6. Rewrite with It was not until: *I didn't understand grammar until I was twenty.* → It was not until I was twenty that I understood grammar.
7. Complete for a research paper: *______ these findings suggest is that…* → What
8. Complete for a research paper: *______ remains unclear is…* → What
9. In Leila's talk, which children kept trying after failure? → It was the children praised for effort who kept trying.
10. Complete: *She ______ her success to her first teacher.* → attributes
11. Complete: *Good teachers ______ curiosity.* (help it develop) → foster
12. Complete: *My grandmother ______ a love of reading in me.* → instilled
13. Complete: *______ hindsight, it was the best decision of my life.* → In
14. Why is one study rarely enough? → Results need to be repeated with other people and in real conditions; later research on mindset found smaller effects than the early studies suggested.

## 0. The lesson infographic: The First Few Seconds

We make snap judgements about people almost instantly. Here is what three classic studies found, and why we should be careful. (All four studies are real.)

**Order: Asch (1946), Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology.** Participants heard six words describing a person. One group heard *intelligent, industrious, impulsive, critical, stubborn, envious*. Another group heard the same words in reverse order. The first group formed a much more positive impression. The first words shaped how people understood the rest. This is called the **primacy effect**.

**30 seconds: Ambady & Rosenthal (1993), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.** Strangers watched silent video clips of university teachers, about 30 seconds long, and rated them. Their ratings significantly predicted the teachers' end-of-semester evaluations from real students. Later clips of only a few seconds gave similar results. Body language can convey a lot before we say a word.

**0.1 seconds: Willis & Todorov (2006), Psychological Science.** Participants saw photos of faces for just 100 milliseconds. Their judgements of how trustworthy a face looked were very similar to judgements made with no time limit. With more time, people became more confident, but their opinions hardly changed.

**But… Todorov et al. (2015), Annual Review of Psychology.** Fast is not the same as accurate. A major review concluded that impressions based on faces are consistent, because many people agree, but they are often wrong. First impressions can change, but it takes time and new information.

### What this means for your three-minute talk

- Your opening will make a lasting impression, so rehearse it until it feels natural.
- Audiences decide quickly whether you come across as credible and approachable.
- Use your voice, your pauses and your body language to convey confidence.
- An engaging hook, such as a question, a surprising fact or a short story, wins attention in the first ten seconds.
- Be concise: in three minutes, one clear idea per part is enough.
- Tailor your story to your audience. What do they already know?

### Thinking like a researcher

- **Correlation is not proof.** In Ambady and Rosenthal's study, strangers' ratings predicted student evaluations. That does not prove the strangers were *right* about the teachers: both groups may share the same biases (warmth, confidence, attractiveness), and student evaluations are opinions, not a direct measure of good teaching.
- **Artificial vs realistic.** Asch used a list of words, not a real person. That is a limitation (real life is more complex), but also a strength: the researcher changed only one thing, the order.
- **Consistent but often wrong.** If many interviewers agree about a candidate, they may all still be wrong. This is one reason companies use structured interviews.

## 1. The model talk: "Car Parks and Floods"

Context: The International Students' Society runs a Three-Minute Stories night. Daniel Okafor, a master's student in environmental engineering, tells the story of his journey so far. (Daniel is fictional.)

1. Good evening, everyone. Let me start with a question. How long does it take you to decide what you think of someone? Research suggests it's probably less than a second. So I have three minutes to change your first impression of me. Let's see if I can do it.

2. My name is Daniel Okafor, and I'm doing a master's in environmental engineering here. I'd like to tell you my story in three parts: where I started, what shaped me, and where I'm going.

3. So, let's begin with where I started. I grew up in Lagos, in Nigeria, in a neighbourhood where the streets flooded every rainy season. I majored in civil engineering because I was good at maths, not because I was passionate about it. After I graduated in 2018, I spent four years designing car parks. I gained a lot of experience, but I wasn't proud of my work.

4. That brings me to the second part: what shaped me. It wasn't a book or a class that changed my mind. It was a flood. In 2020, the water came into my parents' house, and my mother lost everything on the ground floor. Standing in that water, I asked myself a simple question: why am I designing car parks when my own street can't handle the rain? In hindsight, that was the most pivotal moment of my life. What I realised was that engineering is not about concrete. It's about people.

5. Now let me move on to where I am today. Since then, I've made the transition from civil to environmental engineering. I've broadened my research interests to include urban drainage and climate adaptation, and to date, I've worked on two projects on flood risk in West African cities. It hasn't been easy. I failed my first statistics module here, which was a real setback. But it taught me resilience.

6. So, where am I going? I aspire to work with city governments to design streets that absorb water instead of flooding. The reason I'm here tonight is that I believe engineers need to tell stories, not just show numbers.

7. To sum up: I started with car parks, a flood shaped me, and now I want to help cities live with water. If you remember just one thing, let it be this: the best engineering starts with listening to people. Thank you. I'd be happy to answer any questions.

Structure: a question (hook) → a plan in three parts → past (past simple) → what shaped him (clefts) → present and future (present perfect, aspire to) → summary and questions.

## 2. Language guide: signposting

Signposts are phrases that don't add information. They guide the listener: where we are, where we're going, and what matters most. A good short talk follows one simple rule: *Tell them what you'll tell them. Tell them. Tell them what you told them.*

| Function | Story talk | Academic presentation |
|---|---|---|
| Open | Let me start with a question. | In this talk, I'll present… |
| Outline | I'd like to tell you my story in three parts. | I'll start by… Then I'll move on to… Finally, I'll… |
| Move on | That brings me to… · Now let me move on to… | Let me now turn to… · This brings me to my second point. |
| Refer to visuals | – | As you can see on this slide, … · This graph shows… |
| Emphasise | What I realised was… · It wasn't X. It was Y. | What our data suggest is that… · It is X, rather than Y, that… |
| Close | To sum up… · If you remember just one thing, let it be this: … | To sum up / In conclusion, … · The main implication is… |
| Questions | I'd be happy to answer any questions. | I'd be happy to take any questions. |

### Three rules

1. **Announce, then deliver.** Give the number of parts and keep the promise.
2. **Pause after a signpost.** The signpost says a new part is starting; the pause gives the listener time to follow.
3. **Don't over-signpost.** In three minutes, one signpost per part is enough.

### Model: an academic conference opening (fictional study)

> Good morning. In this talk, I'll present a study of flood risk in three neighbourhoods of Lagos. I'll start by describing the problem. Then I'll move on to our method, and finally, I'll discuss what our results mean for city planners. … As you can see on this map, the three areas have very different drainage systems. This brings me to our main finding. What our data suggest is that the oldest drains are not the main problem. It is the lack of green space that makes flooding worse. … To sum up, small parks may protect cities better than bigger pipes. I'd be happy to take any questions.

### Common mistakes (especially for Portuguese speakers)

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Resuming, … | **To sum up**, … · In short, … | False friend: *resume* means start again after a pause. |
| I'd be happy to answer to any questions. | I'd be happy to answer any questions. | *answer* has no preposition. |
| Now I will talk about… | Let me move on to… | Correct, but flat. Vary your signposts. |
| é… né? tipo… | (a pause) · Well, … · Let me think about that. | Fillers make you sound less confident. |

### Pronunciation: chunks, pauses, stress

- Good **EVE**ning, everyone. || Let me start with a **QUES**tion. || How **LONG** does it take you | to decide what you **THINK** of someone?
- That brings me to the **SEC**ond part: | what **SHAPED** me. || It wasn't a **BOOK**. | It was a **FLOOD**.

## 3. Vocabulary

### Lesson vocabulary: making an impression

| Expression | Meaning | Pattern / example |
|---|---|---|
| a snap judgement | a very quick opinion, made without much thought | *We make snap judgements about people.* make a snap judgement about someone (US: judgment) |
| convey | communicate an idea or feeling | *Body language can convey a lot.* convey confidence / a message / emotion |
| make a lasting impression | be remembered for a long time | *Your opening will make a lasting impression.* make a good / strong impression on someone |
| rehearse | practise something before you do it in public | *Rehearse your opening five times.* a rehearsal (n.) |
| come across as | seem to be; give other people this impression | *She comes across as confident.* + adjective / noun |
| credible | easy to believe and trust | *a credible speaker / source* · credibility (n.) |
| approachable | friendly and easy to talk to | *an approachable teacher* |
| engaging | interesting, so people want to keep listening | *an engaging hook / speaker / story* |
| concise | saying a lot in few words | *Be concise.* a concise answer / summary |
| tailor X to Y | change something so it is right for a particular person or group | *Tailor your story to your audience.* |

Need a preposition: come across **as**, tailor X **to** Y, an impression **on** someone. Close to *be perceived as* (Lesson 1): come across as.

## 4. Module 1 review

### Questions (Lesson 1)
- Word order: *Where **are you** from?* (not *Where you are from?*)
- Subject questions have no do / did: *Who **told** you about this job?*
- Polite questions use statement word order: *Could you tell me where the room **is**?*

### My journey (Lesson 2)
- Past simple for finished time: *I **graduated** in 2018.*
- Present perfect for "up to now": *To date, I**'ve published** two papers. I**'ve worked** here since 2022.*
- *I've lived in Lisbon for five years* (I still live there) vs *I lived in Lisbon for five years* (finished).

### Clefts (Lesson 3)
- *It was my mother **who** taught me.* (never drop *It*)
- *What I need **is** more practice.* (no extra *it*)
- *It was not until 2012 **that** I saw a coral reef.*

## 5. Self-check questions (with answers)

1. What did Asch's study show? → The order of information changes our impression: the first words colour the rest (the primacy effect).
2. Why were Ambady's video clips silent? → To measure body language alone, without the content of what teachers said.
3. Does "significantly predicted" mean the strangers were right? → No. It shows a relationship, not that the judgements were accurate.
4. Complete: *That ______ me to my second point.* → brings
5. Correct: *Resuming, reefs can recover.* → To sum up, reefs can recover.
6. Make it suitable for a conference: *OK, that's it. Any questions?* → To sum up, … Thank you. I'd be happy to take any questions.
7. Complete: *She comes ______ as very approachable.* → across
8. Complete: *______ your talk to your audience.* (change it so it fits) → Tailor
9. Complete: *Keep each answer ______: two minutes maximum.* → concise
10. Correct: *I have finished my PhD in 2021.* → I finished my PhD in 2021.
11. Correct: *Could you tell me where is the library?* → Could you tell me where the library is?
12. Correct: *What I love it is working at night.* → What I love is working at night.

The First Few Seconds: what research says about first impressions

We make snap judgements about people almost instantly. Here is what three classic studies found, and why we should be careful.

Order Asch (1946) · Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology

Participants heard six words describing a person. One group heard intelligent, industrious, impulsive, critical, stubborn, envious. Another group heard the same words in reverse order. The first group formed a much more positive impression. The first words shaped how people understood the rest.

30 sec Ambady & Rosenthal (1993) · Journal of Personality and Social Psychology

Strangers watched silent video clips of university teachers, about 30 seconds long, and rated them. Their ratings significantly predicted the teachers' end-of-semester evaluations from real students. Later clips of only a few seconds gave similar results. Body language can convey a lot before we say a word.

0.1 sec Willis & Todorov (2006) · Psychological Science

Participants saw photos of faces for just 100 milliseconds. Their judgements of how trustworthy a face looked were very similar to judgements made with no time limit. With more time, people became more confident, but their opinions hardly changed.

But… Todorov et al. (2015) · Annual Review of Psychology

Fast is not the same as accurate. A major review concluded that impressions based on faces are consistent, because many people agree, but they are often wrong. First impressions can change, but it takes time and new information.

What this means for your three-minute talk

  • Your opening will make a lasting impression, so rehearse it until it feels natural.
  • Audiences decide quickly whether you come across as credible and approachable.
  • Use your voice, your pauses and your body language to convey confidence.
  • An engaging hook, such as a question, a surprising fact or a short story, wins attention in the first ten seconds.
  • Be concise: in three minutes, one clear idea per part is enough.
  • Tailor your story to your audience. What do they already know?

All four studies are real. The tips are general presentation advice.

Second listening · details

1Daniel chose civil engineering because he loved it.
2A flood at his parents' house was the pivotal moment.
3Daniel's master's has been easy so far.

1Good evening, everyone. Let me start with a question. How long does it take you to decide what you think of someone? Research suggests it's probably less than a second. So I have three minutes to change your first impression of me. Let's see if I can do it.

2My name is Daniel Okafor, and I'm doing a master's in environmental engineering here. I'd like to tell you my story in three parts: where I started, what shaped me, and where I'm going.

3So, let's begin with where I started. I grew up in Lagos, in Nigeria, in a neighbourhood where the streets flooded every rainy season. I majored in civil engineering because I was good at maths, not because I was passionate about it. After I graduated in 2018, I spent four years designing car parks. I gained a lot of experience, but I wasn't proud of my work.

4That brings me to the second part: what shaped me. It wasn't a book or a class that changed my mind. It was a flood. In 2020, the water came into my parents' house, and my mother lost everything on the ground floor. Standing in that water, I asked myself a simple question: why am I designing car parks when my own street can't handle the rain? In hindsight, that was the most pivotal moment of my life. What I realised was that engineering is not about concrete. It's about people.

5Now let me move on to where I am today. Since then, I've made the transition from civil to environmental engineering. I've broadened my research interests to include urban drainage and climate adaptation, and to date, I've worked on two projects on flood risk in West African cities. It hasn't been easy. I failed my first statistics module here, which was a real setback. But it taught me resilience.

6So, where am I going? I aspire to work with city governments to design streets that absorb water instead of flooding. The reason I'm here tonight is that I believe engineers need to tell stories, not just show numbers.

7To sum up: I started with car parks, a flood shaped me, and now I want to help cities live with water. If you remember just one thing, let it be this: the best engineering starts with listening to people. Thank you. I'd be happy to answer any questions.

Three rules

1 · Announce, then deliver. Give the number of parts and keep the promise.

2 · Pause after a signpost. The signpost tells the listener a new part is starting; the pause gives them time to follow.

3 · Don't over-signpost. In three minutes, one signpost per part is enough. Be concise.

Common mistakes

I'd be happy to answer to any questions. → answer any questions (no to)
Resuming, … (false friend: resumir) → To sum up, … · In short, …
Now I will talk about… (correct, but flat) → Let me move on to…

Say it naturally

Speak in chunks. Pause after signposts. Stress the key words.

Good EVEning, everyone. || Let me start with a QUEStion. || How LONG does it take you | to decide what you THINK of someone? ||

That brings me to the SECond part: | what SHAPED me. || It wasn't a BOOK. | It was a FLOOD.

It Doesn't Hurt to Ask

Adapted from research by Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson & Gino · Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2017

¶1Most of us assume that the key to a good first impression is to talk about ourselves in an interesting way. Research, however, suggests that the opposite may be true. In a series of studies at Harvard Business School, psychologists analysed hundreds of conversations between people who were meeting for the first time, both in online chats and at face-to-face speed-dating events.

¶2The researchers found that participants who asked more questions were perceived as significantly more likeable by their conversation partners. Not all questions, though, had the same effect. The strongest effect came from follow-up questions: questions that build directly on what the other person has just said, such as “What made you choose that?” In the speed-dating data, people who asked more follow-up questions were more likely to be offered a second date.

¶3Why do follow-up questions work so well? According to the authors, they signal responsiveness. They show that the listener has understood, cares about the answer and wants to know more.

¶4Despite these benefits, people do not seem to realise how much asking questions helps, and many of us ask too few. One explanation is that we are often busy planning what we want to say next instead of listening. The practical implication is simple: if you want to build rapport, be curious first and interesting second.

Prepositions

Where are you from? · What does your research focus on? · Who are you working with?

In speech, the preposition goes at the end. In very formal writing you may see it first: With whom are you working?

Subject questions

When who or what is the subject (the doer), there is no do / does / did. The order is like a statement.

About the subjectAbout the object
Who brought it?What did the coordinator bring?
What made you choose this university?Which university did you choose?
Who has influenced you most?Who have you worked with?
Polite and tentative questions

After these frames, use statement order and no do / does / did:

  • Could you tell me what your thesis is about?
  • Do you know when the seminar starts?
  • I was wondering if / whether you could help me.
  • Would you mind telling me how you got funding?
  • Do you mind if I ask why you left your job?

Yes/no questions use if or whether: Is there a café? → Do you know if there is a café?

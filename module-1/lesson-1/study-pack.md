## 0. The lesson reading: It Doesn't Hurt to Ask

Adapted summary of research by Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson and Gino, published in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology (2017).

Most of us assume that the key to a good first impression is to talk about ourselves in an interesting way. Research, however, suggests that the opposite may be true. In a series of studies at Harvard Business School, psychologists analysed hundreds of conversations between people who were meeting for the first time, both in online chats and at face-to-face speed-dating events.

The researchers found that participants who asked more questions were perceived as significantly more likeable by their conversation partners. Not all questions, though, had the same effect. The strongest effect came from follow-up questions: questions that build directly on what the other person has just said, such as "What made you choose that?" In the speed-dating data, people who asked more follow-up questions were more likely to be offered a second date.

Why do follow-up questions work so well? According to the authors, they signal responsiveness. They show that the listener has understood, cares about the answer and wants to know more.

Despite these benefits, people do not seem to realise how much asking questions helps, and many of us ask too few. One explanation is that we are often busy planning what we want to say next instead of listening. The practical implication is simple: if you want to build rapport, be curious first and interesting second.

### Finding or interpretation?

In academic texts, separate what the researchers **found** (data) from how they **interpret** it (explanations, suggestions).
- Signals of a finding: *found that, the data showed, participants who…, were more likely to…*
- Signals of an interpretation: *according to the authors, one explanation is, this suggests, the implication is*

Examples: "Participants who asked more questions were rated as more likeable" is a finding. "Follow-up questions show that the listener cares" is an interpretation.

## 1. The lesson dialogue: The Welcome Mixer

Context: It's the first week of the semester. The International Office is hosting a welcome event for new postgraduate students. Marina, from Brazil, sits down next to Daniel.

**Marina:** Hi! Is this seat free?
**Daniel:** Sure, go ahead. I'm Daniel, by the way.
**Marina:** Nice to meet you, I'm Marina. So, where are you from?
**Daniel:** Toronto, originally. And you?
**Marina:** Brazil, São Paulo. What are you studying here?
**Daniel:** I'm doing a master's in urban planning. What about you?
**Marina:** Environmental science. I'm researching how cities deal with heat waves.
**Daniel:** No way! That's really close to my area. What made you choose this university?
**Marina:** Mainly the research group. My supervisor's work focuses on climate adaptation. How long have you been here?
**Daniel:** Just two weeks. I'm still getting lost on campus! Who told you about this event?
**Marina:** A girl from my department. She said the food was good. Did she lie?
**Daniel:** Not at all. Have you tried the cheese bread? Someone brought it especially for the Brazilian students.
**Marina:** Seriously? Who brought it?
**Daniel:** I think the coordinator did. So, what do you do when you're not studying?
**Marina:** I play volleyball, and I'm trying to learn to cook properly. Which neighbourhood are you living in?
**Daniel:** Near the river. Why? Are you looking for a place?
**Marina:** Yes! Actually, I was wondering if you could tell me where the housing office is.
**Daniel:** Of course. Come on, I'll show you after we eat.

## 2. Grammar guide

### 2.1 The basic pattern: Q-A-S-I

Most questions in English follow this order:

| Question word | Auxiliary | Subject | Main verb | Rest |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| What | do | you | do | when you're not studying? |
| What | are | you | studying | here? |
| — | Have | you | tried | the cheese bread? |
| Where | did | you | grow up | ? |

Rules:
- The auxiliary always comes **before** the subject.
- Use **do / does / did** only when there is no other auxiliary.
- If the sentence already has **be**, **have** (in perfect tenses) or a modal (**can, could, will, should**), that word moves before the subject. Do not add do.
  - You can swim. → Can you swim? (not "Do you can swim?")
  - She is working. → Is she working?
- With the verb **be** as the main verb, just swap: You are from Toronto. → Where are you from?

### 2.2 Prepositions at the end

In spoken English, the preposition normally stays at the end:
- Where are you **from**?
- What are you working **on**?
- Which neighbourhood are you living **in**?
- What does your research focus **on**?
- Who are you waiting **for**?

### 2.3 Subject questions (no do / does / did)

When **who** or **what** is the subject (the person or thing doing the action), the question has the same order as a statement. There is no do / does / did.

| Statement | Question about the subject | Question about the object |
|---|---|---|
| A girl told Marina. | Who told Marina? | Who did the girl tell? |
| The coordinator brought the cheese bread. | Who brought the cheese bread? | What did the coordinator bring? |
| The research group made her choose this university. | What made her choose this university? | Which university did she choose? |

Quick test: Do you know who did the action? If not, and you are asking about that person, use a subject question.

### 2.4 Polite (indirect) questions

To sound more polite, start with **Could you tell me…**, **Do you know…** or **Do you have any idea…**. After these phrases, use **statement order** and no do / does / did.

- Where is the housing office? → Could you tell me where the housing office **is**?
- When does the library open? → Do you know when the library **opens**?
- What time did the lecture start? → Do you have any idea what time the lecture **started**?
- Is there a café nearby? → Do you know **if** there is a café nearby?
- Can you help me? → I was wondering **if / whether** you could help me.
- What is your thesis about? → Would you mind telling me what your thesis **is** about?
- Why did you leave your job? → Do you mind if I ask why you **left** your job?

### 2.4b Follow-up questions

A follow-up question builds on what the other person has just said. According to the research, it is the type of question that makes people like you most.
- "I've just moved here from Lisbon." → What made you move? / How are you finding it so far?
- "My research focuses on sleep." → What got you interested in that? / What have you found so far?
- Useful starters: What made you…? How did that come about? What happened next? Could you say a bit more about…?

### 2.5 Common mistakes and corrections

| Mistake | Correct |
|---|---|
| Where you are from? | Where are you from? |
| What you are studying? | What are you studying? |
| Do you can help me? | Can you help me? |
| Who did call you? (asking who called) | Who called you? |
| What does your research focus? | What does your research focus on? |
| Could you tell me where is the office? | Could you tell me where the office is? |
| How long you have been here? | How long have you been here? |

### 2.6 Pronunciation

- Wh- questions usually **fall** at the end: Where are you from? ↘
- Yes/no questions usually **rise** at the end: Have you tried it? ↗
- In fast speech, words join: What do you → "whaja"; Did you → "dija"; Have you → "havya".

## 3. Vocabulary

### Lesson vocabulary: talking about your academic path

| Expression | Meaning | Pattern and example |
|---|---|---|
| academic background | your previous studies | have a background in + field: "I have a background in biology." |
| major in | study as your main subject (undergraduate) | "She majored in ecology." |
| specialise in | focus on one specific area | + noun / -ing: "I'm specialising in climate adaptation." |
| pursue | try to achieve over time | + a degree / career / goal: "He's pursuing a PhD." |
| be drawn to | feel attracted to or interested in | + noun / -ing: "I was drawn to studying cities." |
| research interests | the topics you want to investigate | "My research interests include urban heat and public health." |
| interdisciplinary | combining two or more fields | "Her work is interdisciplinary: ecology plus urban planning." |
| a turning point | a moment when things change direction | "A field trip was a turning point in my degree." |
| shape | influence the way something develops | + views / interests / career: "My grandmother shaped my respect for nature." |
| aspire to | hope to achieve something important | + verb / noun: "She aspires to work with city governments." |

Model profile: Marina Alves has an academic background in biology, and she majored in ecology at the University of São Paulo. A field trip to the Amazon was a turning point in her degree: she was drawn to the question of how climate change affects people in cities. Her research interests include urban heat, green spaces and public health. Her work is interdisciplinary, combining ecology, geography and urban planning. Marina is now pursuing a master's in environmental science, specialising in climate adaptation. She says her grandmother, a farmer, shaped her respect for nature, and she aspires to work with city governments on greener neighbourhoods.

Common mistakes: specialise on (✗) → specialise in · aspire becoming (✗) → aspire to become · make a turning point (✗) → be a turning point.

### Research vocabulary
- **participants**: the people who take part in a study.
- **perceive as**: to see or judge someone in a certain way. "They were perceived as more likeable."
- **significantly**: in research, by an amount large enough to be meaningful, not by chance.
- **signal** (verb): to show or suggest something. "Follow-up questions signal responsiveness."
- **rapport**: a friendly connection in which people understand each other. build / establish rapport.
- **implication**: a possible effect or conclusion. "The practical implication is simple."

### Words from the dialogue
- **research** (verb and noun): to study something carefully to find new information. "I'm researching how cities deal with heat waves." / "She does research on coral reefs."
- **focus on**: to give most of your attention to one thing. "My supervisor's work focuses on climate adaptation." Noun: "the focus of my study".
- **area**: a field of study or work. "That's really close to my area." / "My area of interest is public health."
- **adaptation**: the process of changing to fit new conditions. Verb: adapt to. "climate adaptation", "adapting to a new country".
- **department**: a section of a university that teaches one subject. "A girl from my department."
- **supervisor**: the professor who guides a master's or PhD student's research.

### Phrases for conversation
- **…, by the way.** Adds information casually. "I'm Daniel, by the way."
- **And you? / What about you?** Returns the question.
- **No way! / Seriously?** Shows surprise.
- **Same here! / Me neither.** Shows you have something in common.
- **So, …** Changes the topic or starts a new question.
- **Actually, …** Introduces something new or a small correction.

### Phrases for class and seminars
- Asking for clarification: Sorry, could you say that again? / What do you mean by…? / Sorry, I didn't catch that.
- Showing interest: That's really interesting. / How come?
- Buying time: Good question, let me think. / That's a tough one. / Well, it depends…

## 4. More model conversations

### At a conference coffee break

**Ana:** Hi, mind if I join you?
**Prof. Hill:** Not at all. Did you enjoy the morning session?
**Ana:** Very much. Which talk did you like the most?
**Prof. Hill:** The one on urban heat. Who invited you to present?
**Ana:** My supervisor did. She couldn't come, so I'm here instead.
**Prof. Hill:** Lucky you! What's your presentation about?
**Ana:** How green roofs can reduce temperatures in São Paulo. Could you tell me where room B12 is? I think I'm in there at three.
**Prof. Hill:** It's upstairs, next to the library. I'll come and listen.

### First day in a shared flat

**Leo:** Hey, you must be the new flatmate. I'm Leo.
**Bia:** Hi Leo, I'm Bia. Have you lived here long?
**Leo:** About a year. What brought you to the city?
**Bia:** A scholarship, actually. I'm doing a master's in psychology. What do you do?
**Leo:** I work at a bike shop, and I'm studying part-time. Who told you about the room?
**Bia:** A friend from my course. Do you know if there's a supermarket near here?
**Leo:** Yes, round the corner. Come on, I'll show you.

## 5. Self-check questions (with answers)

1. Correct the question: "Where you live?" → Where do you live?
2. Correct the question: "Who did write this article?" (you don't know the writer) → Who wrote this article?
3. Make it polite: "Where is the bus stop?" → Could you tell me where the bus stop is?
4. Put in order: on / are / working / what / you → What are you working on?
5. Answer: "My professor recommended this book." Ask about the person. → Who recommended this book?
6. Answer: "I'm studying chemistry." Write the question. → What are you studying?
7. Why is there no "do" in "What are you studying?" → Because "are" is already the auxiliary.
8. What does "focus on" mean? → To give most of your attention to one thing.
9. What do you say to return a question? → And you? / What about you?
10. Does a wh- question usually go up or down at the end? → Down.
11. What did the Harvard research find? → People who ask more questions, especially follow-up questions, are liked more.
12. Finding or interpretation: "People ask too few questions because they are planning what to say next." → Interpretation.
13. Make it more tentative: "Can you read my abstract?" → I was wondering if you could read my abstract.
14. Correct: "I'm specialising on public health." → I'm specialising in public health.
15. Complete: "A conference in Lisbon was a ______ in my career." → turning point
16. Complete: "She aspires ______ lead her own lab." → to

First listening · the big idea

What is Helena's main message?

RuthWelcome back to Second Acts, the podcast about people who changed direction. My guest today has worked as an architect, a lighting designer and, for the last eight years, a neuroscientist. Helena, welcome.
HelenaThanks, Ruth. When you say it like that, it sounds like I can't make up my mind!
RuthSo let's start at the beginning. What did you study first?
HelenaArchitecture. I graduated in 2009 and spent five years designing hospitals in Lisbon. I gained a lot of experience in a short time, but something was bothering me.
RuthWhat was it?
HelenaWe designed these beautiful buildings, but nobody ever asked how patients actually felt inside them. In 2013 I read a famous study from the 1980s. It showed that patients with a view of trees recovered faster than patients who faced a brick wall. That was a real milestone for me.
RuthSo you decided to switch fields.
HelenaNot immediately. First I made the transition to lighting design, because light affects our mood and our sleep. Then, in 2018, I went back to university to do a PhD in cognitive neuroscience.
RuthThat's a big change. Was it hard?
HelenaVery. In my first year I failed a statistics exam. It was a serious setback, and I almost quit. But then I realised that architects have a lot of transferable skills. We think in three dimensions, we sketch ideas, we manage complex projects.
RuthAnd what have you done since then?
HelenaQuite a lot, actually. I've acquired a completely new set of skills, from brain imaging to coding. To date, I've published eleven papers on how hospital design affects recovery, and I've contributed to new design guidelines for two hospitals.
RuthHave you ever regretted leaving architecture?
HelenaNever. And I haven't really left it. I've just broadened my perspective. Now I design studies instead of buildings.
RuthFinal question. What would you say to a listener who wants to change direction?
HelenaLook at your track record, not your job title. You've probably built more skills than you think.

Past simple

Use it for a finished action at a finished time. The time is said or clear from context: in 2009, last year, two years ago, when I was a student, in my first year.

It tells the story: what happened, step by step.

Present perfect

have / has + past participle. Use it when the time is not finished or not important, and the past connects to now:

  • life experience, no time given: She has worked as an architect.
  • up to now, maybe more later: To date, I've published eleven papers.
  • a result now: I've acquired new skills (I have them now).

Typical words: ever, never, so far, to date, since, for, already, yet, recently.

How the two work together

Speakers often open with the present perfect and continue with the past simple when they give details:

“Have you ever regretted it?” “Yes, once. I regretted it when I failed that exam.”

Think like an academic · Literature reviews

Helena's papers start like this. Why does the tense change?

Introduction · paragraph 1

Over the past four decades, researchers have examined how the physical environment affects patient recovery. In a landmark study, Ulrich (1984) found that surgical patients with a window view of trees left hospital earlier than patients who faced a brick wall. Since then, numerous studies have confirmed the benefits of natural light and green views. However, few studies have investigated the effect of lighting at night. Costa (2023) addressed this gap with a study of 240 patients in two Lisbon hospitals.

Illustrative paragraph. Ulrich (1984) is a real study published in Science; Costa (2023) is fictional.

The academic rule

Present perfect = the state of the field up to now: Research has shown… · Several studies have found… · Few studies have examined…

Past simple = one study with a name and a year: Smith (2019) found… · In a 2020 study, researchers measured…

Signal phrases for the field: over the past decade, in recent years, since then, to date.

Topic: noise in hospital wards

• Buxton et al. (2012), lab study → recorded hospital sounds woke healthy sleepers
• many studies: noise levels above WHO guidelines
• GAP: very few studies on single vs shared rooms at night

Use the present perfect for the field and the past simple for the named study.

Since the 1990s, many researchers have shown that noise disturbs patients' sleep.
Buxton et al. (2012) found that recorded hospital sounds woke healthy sleepers in a laboratory.
Numerous studies have found noise levels above WHO guidelines.
However, very few studies have compared single and shared rooms at night.

Buxton et al. (2012) is a real sleep study published in Annals of Internal Medicine.

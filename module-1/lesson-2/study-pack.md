## 0. The lesson podcast: Second Acts, Episode 14, "From Buildings to Brains"

Context: *Second Acts* is a podcast about people who changed direction. Host Ruth talks to Dr Helena Costa, a cognitive neuroscientist who started her career as an architect. (Helena and the podcast are fictional. The tree study she mentions is real: Ulrich, 1984.)

**Ruth:** Welcome back to Second Acts, the podcast about people who changed direction. My guest today has worked as an architect, a lighting designer and, for the last eight years, a neuroscientist. Helena, welcome.
**Helena:** Thanks, Ruth. When you say it like that, it sounds like I can't make up my mind!
**Ruth:** So let's start at the beginning. What did you study first?
**Helena:** Architecture. I graduated in 2009 and spent five years designing hospitals in Lisbon. I gained a lot of experience in a short time, but something was bothering me.
**Ruth:** What was it?
**Helena:** We designed these beautiful buildings, but nobody ever asked how patients actually felt inside them. In 2013 I read a famous study from the 1980s. It showed that patients with a view of trees recovered faster than patients who faced a brick wall. That was a real milestone for me.
**Ruth:** So you decided to switch fields.
**Helena:** Not immediately. First I made the transition to lighting design, because light affects our mood and our sleep. Then, in 2018, I went back to university to do a PhD in cognitive neuroscience.
**Ruth:** That's a big change. Was it hard?
**Helena:** Very. In my first year I failed a statistics exam. It was a serious setback, and I almost quit. But then I realised that architects have a lot of transferable skills. We think in three dimensions, we sketch ideas, we manage complex projects.
**Ruth:** And what have you done since then?
**Helena:** Quite a lot, actually. I've acquired a completely new set of skills, from brain imaging to coding. To date, I've published eleven papers on how hospital design affects recovery, and I've contributed to new design guidelines for two hospitals.
**Ruth:** Have you ever regretted leaving architecture?
**Helena:** Never. And I haven't really left it. I've just broadened my perspective. Now I design studies instead of buildings.
**Ruth:** Final question. What would you say to a listener who wants to change direction?
**Helena:** Look at your track record, not your job title. You've probably built more skills than you think.

### Helena's timeline

| When | What happened |
|---|---|
| 2009 | Graduated in architecture; started designing hospitals in Lisbon |
| 2013 | Read Ulrich's study on patients and tree views: a milestone |
| 2014 | Made the transition to lighting design |
| 2018 | Started a PhD in cognitive neuroscience |
| 2019 | Failed a statistics exam: a serious setback |
| since then | Has acquired skills in brain imaging and coding |
| now | To date, has published 11 papers; has contributed to design guidelines for two hospitals |

Main message: changing fields is hard, but the skills you already have travel with you.

## 1. Grammar guide

### 1.1 Past simple: the story

Use the past simple for a **finished action at a finished time**. The time is said or clear from context.

- I **graduated** in 2009.
- In my first year, I **failed** a statistics exam.
- She **started** her PhD five years ago.

Typical time expressions: *in 2009, last year, two years ago, yesterday, when I was a student, in my first year, in the 1980s, then, after that*.

### 1.2 Present perfect: the connection to now

Form: **have / has + past participle** (I've worked, she has published, they haven't finished).

Use it when the time is **not finished** or **not important**:

1. **Life experience** (no time given): *She has worked as an architect.* (She isn't an architect now. The sentence is about her experience, not about when.)
2. **Up to now**, more may happen: *To date, I've published eleven papers.*
3. **A result now**: *I've acquired new skills.* (I have them now.)
4. **Unfinished period** with *for / since*: *I've lived here since 2020. I've been a researcher for eight years.*

Typical time expressions: *ever, never, so far, to date, since, for, already, yet, just, recently, over the past ten years, in recent years*.

### 1.3 How the two tenses work together

Speakers often **open** a topic with the present perfect and **continue** in the past simple when they give details:

- A: **Have** you ever **changed** fields?
- B: Yes, I **have**. I **moved** from law to education in 2017.
- A: Why **did** you **do** that?

### 1.4 Tense in academic writing (literature reviews)

| Present perfect | Past simple |
|---|---|
| The **research field** in general, up to now | **One specific study**, with a name and a year |
| *Research has shown that…* | *Ulrich (1984) found that…* |
| *Several studies have examined…* | *In a 2020 study, researchers measured…* |
| *Few studies have investigated…* (a gap) | *Costa (2023) addressed this gap.* |

Model paragraph (illustrative; Ulrich 1984 is real, Costa 2023 is fictional):

> Over the past four decades, researchers **have examined** how the physical environment affects patient recovery. In a landmark study, Ulrich (1984) **found** that surgical patients with a window view of trees **left** hospital earlier than patients who **faced** a brick wall. Since then, numerous studies **have confirmed** the benefits of natural light and green views. However, few studies **have investigated** the effect of lighting at night. Costa (2023) **addressed** this gap with a study of 240 patients in two Lisbon hospitals.

### 1.5 Common mistakes (especially for Portuguese speakers)

Portuguese often uses one past tense (*pretérito perfeito*) where English needs two.

| Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|
| I have graduated in 2015. | I graduated in 2015. | finished time: *in 2015* |
| Since then, I worked on three projects. | Since then, I've worked on three projects. | *since then* = up to now |
| Two years ago, I have moved. | Two years ago, I moved. | *ago* = finished time |
| I never used this software. (= in my life) | I've never used this software. | life experience |
| Did you ever think about it? (= in your life, up to now) | Have you ever thought about it? | life experience |
| Research found that… (= the field in general) | Research has found that… | the field up to now |

**The "When exactly?" test:** if you can answer "When exactly?" with a finished time, use the past simple. If not, or if the time isn't important, use the present perfect.

### 1.6 Pronunciation

- Contractions are normal in speech: *I've published, she's worked, we haven't finished*.
- *She's worked* = she **has** worked. *She's working* = she **is** working.
- *Have* is weak in questions: "Have you ever…?" sounds like "havya EVer".
- Stress the main verb, not *have*: "What have you DONE?"

## 2. Vocabulary

### Lesson vocabulary: telling the story of your path

| Expression | Meaning | Pattern / example |
|---|---|---|
| gain experience | get experience gradually, by doing | *I gained a lot of experience in a short time.* Also: gain knowledge / insight into… |
| a milestone | an important event or stage in a process | *That was a real milestone for me.* reach / mark a milestone |
| make the transition | change from one situation or field to another | *I made the transition from architecture to lighting design.* |
| a setback | a problem that stops or delays progress | *It was a serious setback.* suffer / overcome a setback |
| transferable skills | skills you can use in a different job or field | *Writing and project management are transferable skills.* |
| acquire | gain or learn (formal) | *I've acquired new skills / knowledge / a language.* |
| to date | until now (formal); used with the present perfect | *To date, I've published eleven papers.* |
| contribute to | help to create or achieve something | *I've contributed to new design guidelines.* contribute to + noun / -ing |
| broaden | make wider; include more things | *I've broadened my perspective / horizons.* |
| a track record | your past achievements, seen together | *She has a strong track record in research.* |

Positive: milestone, track record, broaden. Negative: setback. Formal: acquire, to date.

### Phrases for telling your story

- Starting: *I started out as… · Originally, I trained as… · My first job was…*
- Moving forward: *That's when I realised… · Along the way, I've… · Looking back, … · Since then, I've…*
- Ending: *To date, I've… · So far, I've… · Right now, I'm…*

### Phrases for citing research

*Research has shown that… · Several studies have found… · Smith (2019) found that… · Few studies have examined… · Since then, …*

## 3. More model texts

### A "Meet our students" profile (model, 140 words)

> I've always been drawn to the question of how people learn, but my path has been anything but straight. I started out as a lawyer: I graduated in law in 2012 and spent four years at a firm in São Paulo. I gained a lot of experience in negotiation, but in 2016 I volunteered as an English teacher for refugees, and that was a turning point. A year later, I made the transition to education. My first year as a teacher was hard; a class I designed failed completely, and that setback taught me to plan with my students, not for them. Since then, I've acquired a master's degree in applied linguistics and I've contributed to two teacher-training programmes. To date, I've taught more than 300 adults. My legal background has given me transferable skills that I use every day: clear writing and careful listening.

### A short conversation at a conference

**A:** So, have you always worked in public health?
**B:** No, actually. I trained as a nurse. I worked in a hospital for six years.
**A:** What made you change?
**B:** I took a course in epidemiology in 2019, and I loved it. That was a real milestone.
**A:** And how has the transition been?
**B:** Not easy! I've had a few setbacks, but I've learned a lot. So far, I've published two papers.

## 4. Self-check questions (with answers)

1. Correct the sentence: *I have finished my master's in 2020.* → I **finished** my master's in 2020.
2. Correct the sentence: *Since 2021, I worked at the same lab.* → Since 2021, I**'ve worked** at the same lab.
3. Choose: *To date, the team (published / has published) six papers.* → **has published**
4. Choose: *Ulrich (1984) (found / has found) that…* → **found** (one specific study)
5. Choose: *Numerous studies (confirmed / have confirmed) this effect.* → **have confirmed** (the field in general)
6. Why does Ruth say "Helena **has worked** as an architect" if Helena isn't an architect now? → It's life experience; no time is given and the time isn't important.
7. Complete: *I failed an exam. It was a serious s______.* → setback
8. Complete: *Writing and teamwork are t______ skills.* → transferable
9. Complete: *She has a strong t______ r______ in data analysis.* → track record
10. Make a question: you want to know if your partner has lived abroad. → Have you ever lived abroad? (Follow-up: When did you live there? What was it like?)
11. Which time expressions go with the past simple? *since then, in 2019, so far, two years ago, to date, when I was 20* → in 2019, two years ago, when I was 20
12. What is the "When exactly?" test? → If you can answer with a finished time, use the past simple; otherwise use the present perfect.
